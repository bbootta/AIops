# AI oversight for US insurers and asset managers: the NAIC bulletin, the 12-state exam pilot, and the SEC's FY2026 priorities

A two-page briefing for risk, compliance, and actuarial governance leaders. The insurance and asset management pictures are different, so this note treats them separately. Positions are stated as of August 2026.

## For insurers: the bulletin, in brief

The NAIC's Model Bulletin on the Use of Artificial Intelligence Systems by Insurers, adopted in December 2023, asks insurers for a written AI systems (AIS) program; board and senior management accountability; risk controls; validation and bias and error testing of models; and oversight of third-party AI, with responsibility remaining with the insurer.

Adoption has spread. As of mid-2026, roughly 25 states plus the District of Columbia have adopted the bulletin, and four further states (California, Colorado, New York, and Texas) operate their own AI frameworks for insurers. Taken together, roughly 29 jurisdictions have adopted AI-specific regulatory guidance or frameworks that apply to insurers. The bulletin itself is regulatory guidance issued under existing state law rather than a new statute or regulation. These counts continue to move; treat them as approximate and check current adoption against your own state footprint before relying on them.

## The 12-state examination pilot

From March through September 2026, twelve states are piloting the NAIC's AI Systems Evaluation Tool in examinations. The tool structures the review of an insurer's AI governance into four exhibits:

1. the AI inventory;
2. the governance framework;
3. higher-risk AI systems;
4. data controls.

An honest status note: this is a pilot, not a settled exam regime. Feedback from the pilot is expected to inform whether the NAIC adopts the tool, with that decision under consideration at the Fall National Meeting in November 2026. Whether or not your lead state is among the twelve, the four exhibits are a reasonable preview of how an AI-focused exam could organize its questions.

## A preparation checklist for insurers

Practical steps, in our words, organized around the four exhibits:

1. **Keep the AI inventory current and owned.** Every AI system in production, with a named owner, refreshed on a schedule rather than reconstructed when an exam letter arrives.
2. **Write down the governance framework as it actually operates.** Committees, approval paths, and escalation routes, not only the policy document.
3. **Identify higher-risk systems explicitly.** Record which systems you rate as higher risk, why, and what additional controls apply to them.
4. **Attach testing evidence to the systems tested.** Validation results and bias and error testing, with dates and reviewer names, retrievable per system.
5. **Document data controls.** Sources, quality checks, and access restrictions for the data feeding material AI systems.
6. **Cover third-party AI with the same discipline.** The bulletin keeps responsibility with the insurer, so vendor-delivered AI needs the same inventory entry and evidence trail as in-house systems.
7. **Rehearse retrieval.** Pick one material system and time how long it takes to produce its complete evidence set. That number is your readiness metric.

## For asset managers: a different, and lighter, picture

It is worth stating plainly: there is no binding model governance rule for asset managers. The SEC's proposed predictive data analytics rule (S7-12-23) was formally withdrawn on June 12, 2025. What remains active is the SEC's FY2026 examination priorities, announced November 17, 2025, which include the adequacy of policies, procedures, and supervision around AI use; the accuracy of statements about AI capabilities; and the consistency of AI-assisted outputs with client profiles and disclosures.

The practical implication is documentation rather than new obligations. Three artifacts cover most of the ground:

- a written view of where AI is used in the investment process and in client-facing work;
- supervision procedures matched to those uses, with named reviewers;
- marketing and disclosure language checked against what your AI actually does.

There is no deadline forcing this work. The reason to do it anyway is that each artifact is inexpensive to maintain and answers an exam question before it is asked.
